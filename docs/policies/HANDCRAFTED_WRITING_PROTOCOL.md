# Generalised Handcrafted Writing, Redaction, Anti-Aberration, and Anti-Bulk-Generation Protocol

## 1. Purpose

This protocol governs the creation, rewriting, expansion, and refinement of substantial written content where quality, coherence, factual reliability, originality, and stylistic consistency matter.

It can be applied to:

reports;

essays;

documentation;

articles;

product copy;

policies;

proposals;

research synthesis;

technical explanations;

narratives;

guides;

long-form web content;

professional communications;

educational material.

Its central rule is:

The scale of the assignment must never be allowed to reduce the attention given to each individual passage.

The protocol therefore treats writing as a sequence of carefully understood and independently reviewed units rather than as a bulk text-production problem.

# 2. Core writing objective

Every substantial section should perform three broad functions.

## A. Orientation

Help the reader understand:

where they are;

why the section exists;

what question, problem, tension, or need it addresses;

how it relates to what came before;

what they should expect to gain from it.

Orientation may use:

a concrete situation;

a relevant anecdote;

a direct problem statement;

an observation;

a historical event;

a real-world example;

a carefully identified hypothetical case;

a concise framing question.

The opening must serve the content. It must not be decorative filler.

## B. Development

Develop the central idea with enough depth for the reader to follow the reasoning rather than merely accept a conclusion.

Depending on the content, this may require:

definitions;

context;

mechanisms;

evidence;

examples;

comparisons;

counterexamples;

trade-offs;

limitations;

implications;

objections;

edge cases;

implementation details;

links to related ideas.

The writer must not treat naming a concept, asserting a conclusion, or listing facts as equivalent to explaining them.

## C. Consolidation

Close the section in a way that helps the reader retain its essential meaning.

A useful close may:

summarise the central insight;

clarify the practical consequence;

identify what remains uncertain;

connect the section to a broader argument;

prepare the next section;

state the decision or action that follows;

distinguish the important conclusion from incidental detail.

The close should not merely repeat the opening in different words.

# 3. Editorial and redaction standard

Here, “redaction” means substantive revision and refinement.

A complete redaction pass should improve the following dimensions.

## 3.1 Purpose

Every passage should have a clear function.

A paragraph should do at least one of the following:

frame;

define;

explain;

support;

illustrate;

compare;

qualify;

challenge;

connect;

conclude;

direct action.

Paragraphs that perform no identifiable function should be removed or rewritten.

## 3.2 Structure

The sequence of ideas should feel inevitable rather than assembled.

The reader should understand:

why the discussion begins where it does;

how each paragraph follows from the previous one;

why an example appears;

what conclusion the evidence supports;

why a qualification matters;

how the section prepares what follows.

Avoid collections of individually competent paragraphs that do not form a coherent argument.

## 3.3 Completeness

Repair content that:

makes unsupported claims;

omits essential context;

skips reasoning steps;

assumes hidden knowledge;

offers only definitions;

gives instructions without explaining consequences;

presents evidence without interpretation;

recommends action without discussing limitations;

introduces terminology without clarifying it.

Completeness does not mean exhausting every possible detail. It means including what the intended reader needs to understand and use the content responsibly.

## 3.4 Precision

Check that:

nouns refer to identifiable things;

pronouns have clear antecedents;

claims are appropriately bounded;

uncertainty is visible;

examples represent the stated concept;

statistics have denominators and context;

causal language is not used for mere association;

absolute terms such as “always,” “never,” “best,” and “proven” are justified.

Replace persuasive vagueness with inspectable meaning.

## 3.5 Readability

Use varied sentence lengths and natural rhythm.

Avoid:

clipped fragments;

telegraphic prose;

monotonous sentence patterns;

oversized paragraphs;

excessive subordinate clauses;

repeated transitions;

unnecessary nominalisations;

unexplained abbreviations;

jargon used as shorthand for missing reasoning.

Short sentences are allowed. Long sentences are allowed. Each should be used deliberately.

## 3.6 Accessible sophistication

Use precise and expressive language without making the reader decode the prose.

When a specialised term is necessary:

introduce it in context;

define it;

demonstrate how it is being used;

use it consistently thereafter.

Sophistication should come from thought, not ornament.

## 3.7 Tone

The preferred general voice is:

articulate;

warm;

intelligent;

precise;

patient;

confident without arrogance;

elegant without theatricality;

generous without becoming repetitive;

occasionally witty where suitable.

A reusable style instruction is:

Write in a warm, elegant, essayistic voice with precise vocabulary, varied cadence, humane intelligence, and enough context for the reader to follow the reasoning without strain.

# 4. Anti-aberration doctrine

## 4.1 Definition

An aberration is a quality failure caused by scale, repetition, automation pressure, context loss, weak review, or stylistic drift.

Typical aberrations include:

generic openings;

interchangeable paragraphs;

repeated conclusions;

irrelevant anecdotes;

invented facts;

shallow verbosity;

unexplained jargon;

unsupported confidence;

contradictory terminology;

abrupt changes in voice;

content from one section leaking into another;

examples that do not support the claim;

internal process commentary appearing in public-facing text;

formulaic prose that could belong to any topic;

repeated structures caused by templating rather than purpose.

## 4.2 Unit-level authorship

Treat every section or subsection as an individual editorial assignment.

Before writing, establish:

AudiencePurposeReader’s starting knowledgeCore questionRequired conclusion or outcomeSupporting evidenceLikely misunderstandingNecessary exampleImportant limitationRelationship to adjacent sectionsDesired final impression or action

Do not begin drafting before these are sufficiently clear.

## 4.3 Context refresh

Before editing each unit:

reread the current unit;

reread the preceding unit;

inspect the following unit;

identify the document’s larger purpose;

verify terminology already introduced;

check relevant decisions, evidence, and constraints;

review earlier rejected approaches.

This reduces contradiction, repetition, and topic drift.

## 4.4 Factual control

Any factual anecdote, quotation, statistic, historical claim, legal statement, technical assertion, or attributed view must be:

verified;

accurately represented;

relevant;

proportionate to the argument;

cited where appropriate;

clearly marked as uncertain when uncertainty remains.

When verification is unavailable, use a transparent hypothetical example rather than fabricating a real event.

## 4.5 Expansion quality test

Every added sentence should contribute at least one of the following:

information;

reasoning;

evidence;

clarification;

distinction;

illustration;

qualification;

implication;

transition;

synthesis.

Added length that performs none of these functions is not improvement.

## 4.6 Meta-leak control

Public-facing content must not accidentally expose internal production language such as:

prompts;

agents;

audit waves;

quality passes;

ticket numbers;

implementation phases;

generation instructions;

internal reviewer notes;

placeholders;

repository operations;

hidden scoring systems.

Internal process language should remain in working files, not in the finished artifact.

# 5. Anti-bulk-generation doctrine

## 5.1 Prohibited automation

Do not use scripts, loops, templates, substitution systems, or unattended generation mechanisms to mass-produce final prose.

Prohibited patterns include:

looping through headings and inserting each into the same paragraph structure;

generating many introductions by replacing only names or nouns;

using one conclusion formula for every section;

assembling prose from reusable sentence fragments;

adding filler to meet length targets;

paraphrasing large bodies of text automatically without individual review;

producing dozens of sections in one pass and treating file creation as proof of quality;

using generic blurbs that could apply to unrelated subjects.

The concern is not that automation exists. The concern is that automation replaces judgment.

## 5.2 Permitted supporting automation

Automation may assist with mechanical or diagnostic tasks such as:

inventorying sections;

locating missing content;

checking headings;

detecting duplicate phrases;

measuring paragraph similarity;

finding broken links;

checking terminology consistency;

running grammar tools;

calculating readability indicators;

validating schemas;

executing tests;

comparing files;

generating diffs;

tracking completion;

verifying that changes appear in the rendered result.

The governing distinction is:

Automation may locate, measure, organise, and validate writing. It must not substitute for attentive authorship of the final prose.

## 5.3 Small-batch production

Work in small, reviewable batches.

A suitable unit may be:

one section;

one subsection;

one argument;

one closely related group of passages.

For each batch:

understand;

research;

outline;

write;

revise;

compare;

validate;

checkpoint;

continue.

Do not generate an entire long document in one undifferentiated pass when doing so would reduce quality.

## 5.4 Anti-repetition comparison

After finishing each unit, compare it with recent units.

Check for repeated:

opening constructions;

rhetorical questions;

anecdote types;

metaphors;

transitions;

paragraph lengths;

sentence rhythms;

conclusion formulas;

calls to action;

examples.

Repetition is acceptable when structurally necessary. It is not acceptable when it reveals inattentive templating.

## 5.5 No-placeholder rule

The following are hard failures in finished content:

TODO;

“expand later”;

“insert example”;

unfinished headings;

generic filler;

fake citations;

invented evidence;

empty sections;

repeated stock paragraphs;

content that merely restates its heading.

Incomplete work should be identified in an internal status record, not disguised as finished writing.

# 6. Research and evidence protocol

Research should precede writing whenever the work depends on external facts, current practice, legal interpretation, technical accuracy, or a specialised domain.

Research should determine:

what is known;

what is disputed;

what has changed;

which sources are authoritative;

which claims are provisional;

which examples are reliable;

which terminology is current;

which conclusions the evidence does and does not support.

Use source hierarchy appropriate to the task:

primary sources;

official documentation;

standards;

peer-reviewed research;

authoritative institutional guidance;

reputable expert analysis;

secondary commentary;

forums and social posts as problem-discovery inputs, not final authority.

Research should be translated into writing decisions rather than displayed as a decorative list of concepts.

# 7. Multi-role editorial harness

Different responsibilities should be separated where scale or risk justifies it.

## Researcher

Collects and evaluates relevant sources.

Does not approve final prose.

## Structural editor

Defines argument order, section purpose, and information hierarchy.

Does not perform final factual verification alone.

## Primary writer

Produces the draft according to the approved brief.

Does not certify their own work as complete.

## Style editor

Improves rhythm, tone, clarity, transitions, and voice consistency.

Must not beautify inaccurate or unsupported content.

## Fact verifier

Checks claims, evidence, quotations, terminology, and source representation.

Does not silently change the intended argument without recording the issue.

## Anti-aberration reviewer

Searches for:

repetition;

generic prose;

topic drift;

factual invention;

meta-language;

shallow verbosity;

style collapse;

signs of bulk generation.

## Reader advocate

Reviews from the intended audience’s perspective.

Checks comprehension, usefulness, emotional effect, and likely misunderstandings.

## Independent validator

Makes the final pass/fail decision using evidence and explicit criteria.

The validator may reject the artifact but should not quietly rewrite it and then approve their own work.

# 8. Typed editorial handoff

Each writing unit should carry a structured brief.

{  "unitId": "string",  "audience": "string",  "purpose": "string",  "readerStartingPoint": "string",  "coreQuestion": "string",  "requiredOutcome": "string",  "keyClaims": [],  "supportingEvidence": [],  "examples": [],  "limitations": [],  "likelyMisunderstandings": [],  "connectionsBackward": [],  "connectionsForward": [],  "tone": "string",  "risks": [],  "status": "researched|outlined|drafted|revised|verified|approved"}

Incomplete or contradictory handoffs should fail closed rather than being passed downstream silently.

# 9. Required writing loop

For every meaningful unit:

## Round 1 — Diagnose

Determine:

what the current text is trying to accomplish;

what succeeds;

what fails;

what is missing;

what is redundant;

what may confuse or mislead the reader.

## Round 2 — Research

Verify relevant facts, terminology, examples, and current context.

## Round 3 — Plan

Create a compact content map.

For example:

OrientationQuestion or problemCore claimSupporting explanationEvidenceExampleCounterpoint or limitationImplicationConclusion or next action

The exact structure should follow the purpose of the unit rather than a universal template.

## Round 4 — Draft attentively

Write the passage directly and specifically for its subject and audience.

Do not use bulk-generation scripts or generic paragraph templates.

## Round 5 — Substantive edit

Improve:

argument;

completeness;

ordering;

evidence;

distinctions;

limitations;

usefulness.

## Round 6 — Style and redaction pass

Improve:

clarity;

cadence;

vocabulary;

transitions;

paragraph balance;

tone;

concision where repetition remains;

expansion where reasoning is too thin.

## Round 7 — Anti-aberration pass

Check for:

repeated formulas;

generic language;

topic contamination;

invented facts;

meta-leaks;

unexplained jargon;

unsupported claims;

unnecessary verbosity;

abrupt stylistic changes.

## Round 8 — Reader validation

Ask whether the intended reader can:

identify the main point;

follow the reasoning;

distinguish evidence from opinion;

understand the implications;

identify relevant limitations;

know what to think, decide, or do next.

## Round 9 — Rendered validation

Inspect the actual published or formatted result.

Check:

headings;

spacing;

links;

tables;

lists;

typography;

truncation;

duplicated content;

mobile readability;

accessibility;

formatting artifacts.

## Round 10 — Memory and evidence

Record:

sources;

decisions;

defects corrected;

rejected approaches;

remaining uncertainty;

validation evidence;

reusable patterns.

Proceed only after the unit passes its gate.

# 10. General validation rubric

Score each unit from 0 to 3.

## Purpose

0: purpose is unclear

1: purpose is implied but unfocused

2: purpose is clear

3: purpose is clear, necessary, and well fulfilled

## Structure

0: incoherent

1: loosely assembled

2: logically ordered

3: progression feels natural and persuasive

## Substance

0: inaccurate or empty

1: superficial

2: sufficiently developed

3: precise, well-supported, nuanced, and useful

## Readability

0: difficult or broken

1: unnecessarily burdensome

2: clear

3: clear, elegant, well-paced, and accessible

## Voice

0: inconsistent or inappropriate

1: functional but generic

2: consistent and suitable

3: distinctive, controlled, and well matched to audience and purpose

## Original authorship quality

0: placeholder or obvious bulk generation

1: heavily formulaic

2: individually adapted

3: unmistakably crafted for the exact subject and context

## Reliability

0: unsupported or misleading

1: important verification gaps

2: adequately supported

3: carefully verified, bounded, and transparent about uncertainty

Suggested gate:

Minimum 18/21No category below 2Substance, Original Authorship Quality, and Reliability must each score 3

# 11. Verbosity discipline

The protocol favours rich and complete writing, but it must not convert length into a quality metric.

Use productive expansion.

Productive expansion adds:

reasoning;

evidence;

examples;

distinctions;

context;

mechanisms;

limitations;

implications;

transitions.

Unproductive expansion adds:

repeated claims;

decorative adjectives;

unnecessary anecdotes;

multiple metaphors for one point;

lengthy throat-clearing;

conclusions that retell the whole section;

needless restatement.

The governing rule is:

Expand until the reader has enough context to understand the idea responsibly, then stop before explanation becomes repetition.

# 12. Separation of concerns

Permanent writing standards should remain separate from temporary operational instructions.

## Editorial protocol

Covers:

research;

structure;

drafting;

redaction;

style;

anti-aberration;

validation.

## Production protocol

Covers:

file handling;

publishing;

versioning;

approvals;

commits;

deployment;

rollback.

## Access and authentication runbook

Covers:

credentials;

login;

device authorisation;

session persistence;

failure recovery.

Temporary instructions such as authentication steps, branch names, or deployment commands should not be embedded inside the permanent writing standard.

# 13. Hard-failure conditions

Reject the work when:

final prose was mass-produced through a script or template;

multiple sections are substantially interchangeable;

evidence was fabricated;

quotations or anecdotes are invented;

length was added without understanding;

central reasoning steps are absent;

terminology contradicts itself;

examples fail to support the claim;

uncertainty is concealed;

internal process language leaks into public content;

placeholders remain;

tone shifts without purpose;

ornate language obscures meaning;

automated checks replace editorial judgment;

the writer is the sole validator;

file existence is treated as proof of quality;

the rendered result has not been inspected.

# 14. Definition of Done

A written artifact is complete only when:

its purpose and audience are explicit;

each section performs a necessary function;

the argument or information structure is coherent;

important reasoning steps are present;

claims are appropriately supported;

uncertainty and limitations are visible;

terminology is consistent;

examples are relevant and accurate;

no placeholder or filler remains;

no bulk-generated prose remains;

repeated patterns have been reviewed;

internal meta-language has been removed;

tone is consistent;

verbosity is productive;

accessibility and readability have been considered;

links and references work;

the actual rendered result has been inspected;

an independent validator has approved it;

decisions, evidence, and unresolved limitations have been recorded.

# 15. Compact reusable directive

Create or revise each writing unit as an individually considered piece of communication. Establish its audience, purpose, central question, evidence, limitations, relationship to surrounding content, and intended effect before drafting. Orient the reader clearly, develop the substance with enough reasoning and context to be useful, and conclude with an appropriate synthesis, implication, or next action. Write in a warm, precise, elegant voice with varied cadence and accessible sophistication. Never use scripts, loops, templates, filler functions, placeholders, or unattended bulk-generation methods to produce final prose. Automation may support research, inventory, diagnostics, consistency checks, and validation, but every public-facing sentence must be deliberately authored and reviewed. Work in small batches, refresh context before each unit, compare recent passages for repetition, verify factual claims, remove internal process language, inspect the rendered result, and require independent approval before declaring completion.
